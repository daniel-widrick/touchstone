# Symbols, pictures and self-labelling values in data display - intake notes

Purpose: find what the evidence says, for and against, about using symbols, pictures and self-identifying values in place of words when displaying data and status, and about removing labels. Written to test rule COPY-016, which currently rests on the owner's judgement.

### 2.0 to 2.3: labels, abbreviations, conventions

- 2.0/2 | Only Necessary Data Displayed | Show only what the task needs; the source's bad example is a code table with an added column nobody needs. Its comment says extra data can confuse and slow the uptake of what is needed. | declutter | held. The harm named is extra data, not extra labels
- 2.0/3 | Data Displayed in Usable Form | The comment adds that users must not have to consult documentation to learn what a displayed item means. | label-needed | held. Cuts against an unexplained symbol as much as against a cryptic code
- 2.0/4 | Data Display Consistent with User Conventions | Show data in the units, clock, calendar and other formats the users already use. | label-not-needed, symbol-over-words | new. A familiar format is what lets a value be recognised at all
- 2.0/16 | Minimal Use of Abbreviation | Whole words are preferred; abbreviations are allowed when much shorter, space is needed and users will understand them. | label-needed | held. "Temp", "Precip" fall under this: the source would spell them out before it would drop them
- 2.0/17 | Common Abbreviations | Where abbreviating is unavoidable, abbreviate only words whose short form is commonly known and unambiguous, and leave the others whole. | label-needed | new
- 2.0/18 | Simple Abbreviation Rule | Derive abbreviations by one simple rule (truncation is preferred) that users understand. | label-needed | new
- 2.0/21 | Dictionary of Abbreviations | If abbreviations are used, users can look up what each stands for. | legend | new. Same idea as a legend for symbols
- 2.2/3 | Data Field Labeling | Every data field has a label. The comment says not to assume a field will be recognised from familiarity: a phone number is obvious in a directory and may not be in an unlabelled display. | label-needed | held. This is the strongest statement in the source against dropping labels from self-evident values. It also says context does the work, which leaves room for a display whose context is unmistakable
- 2.2/4 | Descriptive Wording of Labels | A label describes the content of its field in a word or phrase chosen to help scanning. | label-needed | new
- 2.2/8 | Distinctive Label Format | Labels look different from data (case, brightness, type) so the two can be told apart. | declutter | new. The source's answer to label clutter is to quieten labels, not delete them
- 2.2/9 | Labels Close to Data Fields | A label sits close enough to its data to be read as belonging to it, with at least a space between. | label-needed | new
- 2.2/10 | Labeling Units of Measurement | The unit is shown either in the label or with each value. | label-not-needed | held. The unit may travel with the value; the item still assumes a label exists
- 2.3/6 | Row and Column Labels | Table rows and columns are labelled to the same standard as form fields. | label-needed | held. Directly covers a row of temperatures in a table
- 2.3/8 | Distinctive Labeling | Row and column labels are distinguishable from the table's data. | declutter | new
- 2.3/11 | Labeling Units of Measurement | In a table the unit goes in the column heading, or after the first entry only; it is not repeated down the column. | redundancy, declutter | held. The one place the source endorses leaving a repeated element out

### 2.4 Graphics

- 2.4/1 | Graphic Displays | Consider graphics in place of text or tables for data about relations in space or time; the comment says people do not readily take in detailed text or tables, and suggests graphics combined with text annotation and the detailed figures. | symbol-over-words | new
- 2.4/2 | Data Comparison | Consider a graphic format when users must scan and compare related sets of data quickly. | symbol-over-words | new. Lead statement read; example not read
- 2.4/3 | Monitoring Data Change | Consider a graphic format when users must watch changing data; better still, have the system flag abnormal values. | symbol-over-words | new. Source of HHS 14:11
- 2.4/4 | Consistency | Keep the logic, format and labelling of each kind of graphic the same throughout. | legend | new
- 2.4/5 | Only Necessary Information Displayed | Give a graphic only the data the task needs; the comment says a simplified schematic can inform better than a realistic picture, and that the right level of detail comes from task analysis. Cites Tufte 1983. | declutter, symbol-over-words | new
- 2.4/6 | Highlighting Critical Data | Mark the critical or abnormal part of a graphic with a distinctive code. | redundancy | new
- 2.4/8 | Text Annotation | Where a graphic has a feature that needs attention, consider adding words to point it out. The comment calls this audiovisual lore: get the message across in words as well as pictures. | label-needed, redundancy | new. Cuts toward adding words
- 2.4/9 | Data Annotation | Where exact reading matters, add the actual values to the graphic; often only selected values need it. | label-needed | new. Source of HHS 14:10
- 2.4/10 | Consistent Annotation Format | Place annotation in the same relation to the graphic elements throughout. | legend | new
- 2.4/11 | Normal Orientation for Labels | Labels on graphics, axis labels included, are set in the normal reading orientation. | label-needed | new
- 2.4/12 | Standard Symbols | Give each graphic symbol one standard meaning and keep it within a system and across systems with the same users. The comment says that where users may not know the symbols, consider a legend, or let users ask for a symbol's definition by pointing at it. | legend, symbol-over-words | new. The point-at-it comment is the 1986 form of naming a symbol on hover
- 2.4/13 | Pictorial Symbols | Make icons and pictograms look like what they stand for, and test the set with representative users. The comment says some pictorial symbols already have conventional meanings in a user population and those must be followed, that new symbols must always be tested, and that a symbol clear to its designer may not be understood by users. | symbol-over-words, label-needed | new. The closest item in the source to COPY-016. It accepts conventional symbols and demands testing of the rest
- 2.4/14 | Simple Texture Codes | Use simple hatching, not elaborate patterns, to code areas. | declutter | new
- 2.4.1/11 | Aids for Scale Interpolation | Where exact reading is needed, provide an aid such as an optional fine grid, movable rules, or a readout of the value of any item the user points at. | declutter, label-not-needed | new. Supports moving detail to an on-request readout
- 2.4.1/12 | Unobtrusive Grids | Grid lines must not look like data or hide it; they are thinner than data lines and can be switched off. | declutter | new. Cites Cleveland 1985 and Tufte 1983
- 2.4.2/2 | Highlighting | Make significant plotted points stand out by a code or by annotation. | redundancy | new
- 2.4.3/3 | Labeling Curves | Label each curve directly beside it instead of through a separate legend; a legend is the fallback when curves are too close. The comment says direct labels are taken in faster. Cites Milroy and Poulton 1978. | legend | new
- 2.4.3/4 | Compatible Ordering in Legends | If a legend is needed, list its entries in the same order as the curves appear. | legend | new
- 2.4.3/6 | Line Coding to Distinguish Curves | Code multiple curves by line type or colour so they can be told apart; where one curve is compared with many, highlight that one. | redundancy | new
- 2.4.3/7 | Consistent Line Codes | The same line code stands for the same data across a series of charts. | legend | new
- 2.4.3/14 | Labeling Surface Charts | Label the bands of an area chart inside the bands where space allows. | legend | new
- 2.4.4/8 | Labeling Paired Bars | Label paired bars directly on one pair instead of using a legend. The comment says a separate legend makes reading slower and more error-prone, and that labelling one pair is probably enough. | legend, declutter | new. "One pair is enough" is another endorsed omission of a repeated label
- 2.4.4/11 | Restricted Use of Icons | Icons of varying size in place of bars to show quantities are for special cases only, where the icon is unambiguous and nobody needs to interpolate. The comment says icons are often ambiguous and so have to be explained somewhere on the figure. | label-needed, legend | new. Against pictures as a measure of quantity
- 2.4.5/2 | Labeling Pie Charts | Label pie segments directly, in normal reading orientation, not by a legend. | legend | new
- 2.4.5/3 | Numeric Labels | Add the number to each pie segment label, since a pie has no scale. | label-needed | new
- 2.4.6/1 | Pictures | Use pictures where an accurate, detailed depiction of an object or process is needed. The comment says simplified line drawings are sometimes understood more readily than photographs. | symbol-over-words | new
- 2.4.6/2 | Diagrams | Use a diagram, limited to what the task needs, where a full picture would be needlessly complicated. | declutter | new
- 2.4.8/4 | Labeling Maps | Label significant map features directly where that does not clutter; otherwise put the labels to one side keyed by symbol, or show a label when the user selects the point. | legend, declutter, label-not-needed | new. Explicit licence to show a label on request when always-on labels would clutter
- 2.4.8/5 | Consistent Positioning of Labels | Place map labels in a consistent position relative to what they name; the comment describes crowded labels hiding features and slowing reading. | declutter | new

### 2.5 and 2.6: format and coding

- 2.5/3 | Spacing to Structure Displays | The comment says tightly packed data are hard to find and read, and related items should still sit close enough to limit eye travel. Cites Tullis 1983. | declutter | held
- 2.6/1 | Highlighting Critical Data | The comment says highlighting works best when sparing, on a display that is otherwise uniform. | declutter | held
- 2.6/3 | Coding by Data Category | Use display coding where users must tell categories of data apart quickly, above all when the items are scattered irregularly. | symbol-over-words | new. Supports a symbol per category for scanning
- 2.6/4 | Meaningful Codes | Choose meaningful or familiar codes over arbitrary ones. | symbol-over-words | new

- 2.6/6 | Definition of Display Codes | When a code is given a special meaning on a display, define it at the bottom of that display, showing the code itself (a map legend; a colour's definition printed in that colour). | legend | new. Base for a legend rule

- 2.6/8 | Alphanumeric Coding | Consider letters and digits as an added code on displays, such as graphics, whose main content is not text. | redundancy | new
- 2.6/11 | Short Codes | Arbitrary codes users must remember are at most four or five characters; meaningful ones may be longer. | label-needed | new
- 2.6/12 | Special Symbols | Consider symbols such as asterisks and arrows to draw attention to items in a text display. | symbol-over-words | new. Symbols as attention markers, not as replacements for names
- 2.6/13 | Consistent Use of Special Symbols | A symbol that signals a critical condition is used for nothing else. | legend | new
- 2.6/14 | Markers Close to Words Marked | A marker symbol is separated from the word it marks by a space. | declutter | new
- 2.6/15 | Shape Coding | Consider geometric shapes to help tell categories apart on graphics; about 15 shapes can be told apart readily. | symbol-over-words | new
- 2.6/16 | Establishing Standards for Shape Coding | Assign shape codes from established standards or conventional meanings. The comment says even mnemonic shapes are read partly through learned association, so users' existing standards must be respected. | symbol-over-words, label-needed | new. States plainly that symbols are learned, not self-evident
- 2.6/21 | Limited Use of Size Coding | Code by size only on displays that are not crowded; two or three sizes is the practical limit. | declutter | new
- 2.6/22 | Adequate Differences in Size | A larger symbol is at least 1.5 times the height of the next smaller. | redundancy | new
- 2.6/23 | Limited Use of Brightness Coding | Treat brightness as a two-level code. The example is dim labels with bright data to help scanning. | declutter | new. Again: quieten the label, keep it
- 2.6/25 | Color Coding for Relative Values | A gradual colour scale suits relative values of one variable; people cannot tie a shade to an absolute value. | label-needed | new. A colour scale alone cannot stand for a number
- 2.6/26 | Color Coding for Data Categories | Consider one colour per category for fast discrimination of scattered items; keep to about five. | symbol-over-words | new
- 2.6/27 | Easily Discriminable Colors | Category colours must be easy to tell apart, and should be tested with users in realistic conditions. | redundancy | new
- 2.6/29 | Adding Color to Formatted Displays | Design the display to work in monochrome first, then add colour. | redundancy | new
- 2.6/30 | Redundant Color Coding | Colour is always backed by another feature such as a symbol. | redundancy | held. Redundancy the source requires, the opposite of redundancy to remove
- 2.6/31 | Unique Assignment of Color Codes | Each colour stands for one category only. | legend | new
- 2.6/32 | Conventional Assignment of Color Codes | Assign colours by their conventional associations (red danger, yellow caution, green normal); the comment says other meanings can be learned if applied consistently. | symbol-over-words | new. The associations are described as belonging to "our society", so not universal

## D. Research and standards

Each entry: citation; URL; how it was read; finding; which way it cuts; how far it applies to data display on screens.

### Pictures versus words in perception and memory

- Paivio and Csapo, 1973, Cognitive Psychology 5 (picture superiority, dual coding); Nelson, Reed and Walling, 1976, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory 2. URL: none retrieved for the papers. Read: secondary only (HHS 14:15 comment and source list, and search summaries of encyclopaedia articles). Finding: items studied as pictures are recalled and recognised later better than items studied as words; Nelson et al. attribute this to pictures being more distinct from one another, and report that the advantage shrinks or reverses when the pictures are visually or conceptually similar `?`. Cuts: supports symbol over words, for memory. Applies: weakly. It is about remembering what was seen, not about how fast or how accurately a symbol on a display is understood. Useful for symbols people return to daily.
- Potter and Faulconer, 1975, Time to understand pictures and words, Nature 253, 437-438. URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/253437a0 (not readable; PubMed record has no abstract). Read: secondary (search summaries of citing papers). Finding: a written word is named faster than a picture of the same object, while deciding what category the thing belongs to is as fast for pictures as for words, or slightly faster `?`. Cuts: mixed. Applies: moderately. It says a clear picture of a familiar object reaches its meaning about as fast as its word, not markedly faster. It does not support "a raindrop is read faster than the word" as a general statement.

### Symbols versus text in the field

- Ells and Dewar, 1979, Rapid comprehension of verbal and symbolic traffic sign messages, Human Factors 21(2), 161-168. URL: https://scilit.com/publications/9be014d303ddd2f7f29d921b932094e7 (HTTP 403 on fetch). Read: search summary of the abstract. Finding: road signs carrying a symbol were understood faster than signs carrying words, and degrading the image hurt the worded signs more than the symbolic ones. Cuts: supports symbol over words. Applies: moderately. The symbols were standard road signs, learned and tested by licensing, seen briefly. This is the best direct support found for claim 1, and it is for a trained symbol set.
- Harley (Nielsen Norman Group), 2016, Visual Indicators to Differentiate Items in a List. URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-indicators-differentiators/ . Read: article, through a summarising fetch. Finding: 96 users on mobile pages (two product lists, two stock tables) had to find the three items carrying a given marker. Markers differed by text only, colour only, icon only, or colour plus icon. Text only was slowest (about 22 s per task against 14 s for colour plus icon; first correct click 14 s against 9 s) and least accurate (76% against 85%). Icon alone was marginally better than colour alone, most clearly for up and down arrows on stock tables. Cuts: supports symbol over words. Applies: strongly, with limits. It is the only study found that tests symbols against words for status in a screen list. It is not peer reviewed, covers two states per page, and measures locating items by a marker, not understanding what an unfamiliar marker means. The fetch could not confirm whether the non-text conditions carried any text at all `?`.
- Wiedenbeck, 1999, The use of icons and labels in an end user application program, Behaviour and Information Technology 18(2). Already held as wiedenbeck1999. Read: abstract summary and the account in Leung et al. 2011. Finding: novices using a mail program did as well with labels alone as with icon plus label, and worse with icons alone; icon plus label was rated easiest. Cuts: supports adding a label. Applies: to controls, weakly to data display.
- Leung, McGrenere and Graf, 2011, Age-related differences in the initial usability of mobile device icons, Behaviour and Information Technology 30(5), 629-642. URL: https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/edapt/papers/leung2011.pdf . Read: the paper. Finding: 36 adults, half aged 65 and over, interpreted existing phone icons. Meaning was given correctly for 45% of icons shown alone, 78% with a label and 79% for the label alone. Icons whose picture is closely tied to its meaning scored 84% against 51% for distant ones. Older adults interpreted 38% of unlabelled icons against 52% for younger adults (71% and 86% with labels). Labels helped both age groups equally. Cuts: supports adding a label. Applies: moderately. The icons were for functions, on first encounter. The semantic-closeness result supports the owner's distinction: a picture of the thing itself does far better than a metaphor. Even so, the close set reached 84%, not 100%.
- Isherwood, McDougall and Curry, 2007, Icon identification in context: the changing role of icon characteristics with user experience, Human Factors 49(3), 465-476. URL: https://doi.org/10.1518/001872007X200102 . Read: abstract (PubMed). Finding: icon characteristics explained up to 69% of the variance in identification speed and accuracy; closeness of picture to meaning matters most at first, familiarity matters most with experience, and concreteness matters less than assumed. Cuts: mixed. Applies: well. It says the property that makes a symbol work is that people already know it, which is a fact about the audience and not about the drawing.

### Comprehension of public and safety symbols

- ISO 9186-1:2014 (test method for comprehensibility of graphical symbols), ISO 7001 (public information symbols), ANSI Z535.3 (criteria for safety symbols). URL: https://www.iso.org/standard/59226.html (catalogue page only). Read: not read; secondary only, plus the IIID protocol below. Finding: symbols are accepted into these standards by testing, not by designer judgement. ANSI's criterion is 85% correct with no more than 5% critical confusions (confirmed in the IIID protocol). A lower ISO criterion of about two thirds is widely reported `?` (seen once, as 66%, in a search snippet). Cuts: supports adding a label unless tested. Applies: as method. The bodies that maintain "universal" symbols do not assume any symbol is understood; they measure it.
- IIID / ANSI, 1991, Symbol Comprehension Testing Protocol. URL: https://www.iiid.net/wp-content/uploads/Symbol-Comprehension-Testing-Protocol.pdf . Read: background pages of the document. Finding: states the 85% and 5% criteria and adds that some messages (radiation, biohazard, radio frequency, laser) are unlikely to reach that level in the general population. Cuts: supports adding a label. Applies: as a benchmark for "widely understood".
- Hancock, Rogers, Schroeder and Fisk, 2004, Safety symbol comprehension: effects of symbol type, familiarity, and age, Human Factors 46(2), 183-195. URL: https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.2.183.37344 . Read: abstract (PubMed). Finding: 52 younger and 52 older adults saw 40 safety symbols; comprehension in both groups fell below the 85% criterion, older adults did worse, prohibition symbols were understood best and hazard symbols worst, and familiarity correlated with comprehension. Cuts: supports adding a label. Applies: moderately. Published, standardised symbols are still misread by many people, more so by older people.
- Wolff and Wogalter, 1998, Comprehension of pictorial symbols: effects of context and test method, Human Factors 40(2), 173-186. URL: https://doi.org/10.1518/001872098779480433 (not fetched). Read: search summary of the abstract. Finding: showing the setting in which a symbol would be met raised comprehension; multiple-choice tests with implausible wrong answers inflated scores by about 30% `?`. Cuts: mixed. Applies: well. Context does real work, which supports symbols inside an unmistakable context such as a forecast, and warns that casual checks overstate how well a symbol is understood.
- Zender and Cassedy, 2014, (Mis)understanding: icon comprehension in different cultural contexts, Visible Language 48(1). URL: https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5880 . Read: abstract page, through a summarising fetch. Finding: of 54 "universal" medical icons compared between rural Tanzania and the United States, most were not understood in both; of 47 poorly understood icons, 33 failed for lack of subject knowledge or unfamiliarity with the technology shown and 5 for cultural reasons. Cuts: supports adding a label. Applies: moderately. The main cause of failure is not knowing the thing depicted, which is why depictions of weather and common objects are a better bet than depictions of procedures or devices.
- AIGA/DOT symbol signs, 1974. Read: nothing found. No finding recorded.

### Weather symbols and forecast values

- Reed, Jasko and Senkbeil, 2021 or 2022 `?`, Weather Forecast Semiotics: Public Interpretation of Common Weather Icons, Weather, Climate, and Society (manuscript WCAS-D-21-0067.1). URL: https://geography.ua.edu/departmental-news/weather-forecast-semiotics-public-interpretation-of-common-weather-icons/ (journal page HTTP 403). Read: abstract-level summary only. Finding: 6,253 survey responses on 11 icons used by broadcast meteorologists; people were asked whether each icon fitted common forecast phrases and what chance of precipitation it implied. The background states that viewers infer likelihood, intensity or duration from an icon and that it is not known how well this matches what the forecaster meant. Results not read. Cuts: mixed, leaning to adding a number or word. Applies: directly. A weather icon names the kind of weather well; it does not carry how likely, how much or how long.
- Gigerenzer, Hertwig, van den Broek, Fasolo and Katsikopoulos, 2005, "A 30% chance of rain tomorrow": how does the public understand probabilistic weather forecasts?, Risk Analysis 25(3), 623-629. URL: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.2005.00608.x . Read: abstract (PubMed). Finding: pedestrians in five cities were asked what a 30% chance of rain means; only in New York did a majority give the meteorological meaning, and in the European cities the commonest readings were rain for 30% of the time or over 30% of the area. Cuts: supports adding a label. Applies: directly to the owner's example. A percentage beside a raindrop does not identify itself: it may be read as probability, duration, area, or humidity.
- Joslyn, Nadav-Greenberg and Nichols, 2009, Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society `?`. URL: https://www.washington.edu/news/2009/04/14/peoples-misperceptions-cloud-their-understanding-of-rainy-weather-forecasts (not fetched). Read: search summary of press accounts. Finding: about half of over 450 students misread a chance-of-rain forecast shown with icons; adding an explicit statement of the chance of no rain reduced errors `?`. Cuts: supports adding words. Applies: directly.

### Clutter, redundancy and data-ink

- Tufte, 1983, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information. Read: secondary accounts only; also cited by sm86 at 2.4/5, 2.4/7, 2.4/8, 2.4/11, 2.4/14, 2.4.1/3, 2.4.1/12 and by HHS 14:10, 16:6. Finding as reported: maximise the share of a graphic's ink that shows data, erase non-data ink and redundant data ink, within reason. Cuts: supports removal as a principle. Applies: it is a designer's principle for statistical graphics, not a finding.
- Gillan and Richman, 1994, Minimalism and the syntax of graphs, Human Factors 36(4). URL: https://www.scilit.com/publications/f0343ee3f7188c5a2b262224133fc5e7 (HTTP 403). Read: search summary of the abstract. Finding: across four experiments, graphs with a higher data-ink ratio were sometimes read faster and more accurately, but the effect of removing any one element depended on its place and function, the graph type and the task `?`. Cuts: mixed. Applies: well. Removal helps when the thing removed did no work.
- Blasio and Bisantz, 2002, A comparison of the effects of data-ink ratio on performance with dynamic displays in a monitoring task, International Journal of Industrial Ergonomics 30(2), 89-101. Read: search summary of the abstract. Finding: reactions to out-of-range events were fastest with the highest data-ink ratio; no effect on situation awareness `?`. Cuts: supports removal. Applies: well, it is a status-monitoring display.
- Inbar, Tractinsky and Meyer, 2007, Minimalism in information visualization: attitudes towards maximizing the data-ink ratio, ECCE 2007, 185-188. URL: https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/minimalism-in-information-visualization-attitudes-towards-maximiz/ (not fetched). Read: search summary of the abstract. Finding: people clearly preferred an ordinary bar graph to Tufte's minimalist version. Cuts: against removal, on preference. Applies: to charts.
- Bateman, Mandryk, Gutwin, Genest, McDine and Brooks, 2010, Useful Junk? The effects of visual embellishment on comprehension and memorability of charts, CHI 2010. URL: https://vis.csail.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Bateman-UsefulJunk.pdf . Read: the paper (method and results). Finding: 20 participants; pictorially embellished charts were described as accurately as plain ones, recalled no better minutes later except for the chart's message, and recalled better after two to three weeks. The authors do not recommend embellishment as a general strategy. Cuts: against "removal always helps"; mildly for pictures. Applies: partly. Small sample, a few hand-made charts. It shows a relevant picture need not cost accuracy.
- Ajani, Lee, Xiong, Knaflic, Kemper and Franconeri, 2021, Declutter and Focus: Empirically Evaluating Design Guidelines for Effective Data Communication, IEEE TVCG `?`. URL: https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10350268 . Read: abstract, method and limitations. Finding: 24 undergraduates; decluttered charts (gridlines, excess value labels and colours removed, legends turned into direct labels) were rated more professional but gave no clear memory gain on their own; adding a headline, one highlight colour and annotation improved ratings and memory for the highlighted pattern. The paper calls direct labels in place of legends one of the less disputed steps and says other removals carry trade-offs. Cuts: mixed. Applies: well. Removal alone bought appearance; added words (headline, annotation) bought understanding.
- Rosenholtz, Li and Nakano, 2007, Measuring visual clutter, Journal of Vision 7(2):17; and Rosenholtz et al. 2005 as cited by HHS 6:1. URL: https://doi.org/10.1167/7.2.17 (not fetched). Read: secondary. Finding: measured clutter predicts how long it takes to find a symbol on a map `?`; HHS reports that people agree on which displays are cluttered. Cuts: supports removal of what is not needed. Applies: well to scanning.
- Halverson and Hornof, 2004, as reported in HHS 6:6. Read: secondary (HHS comment). Finding: targets in sparse regions are looked at first and found faster than in crowded regions. Cuts: supports removal. Applies: well.
- Resnick and Fares, 2004; Bandos and Resnick, 2004, as reported in HHS 11:9 and 11:11. Read: secondary (HHS comments). Finding: a colour code helped only when its meaning was explained (40% gain with both); two simultaneous highlighting schemes halved the gain of one. Cuts: supports a legend, and supports removing a second redundant cue. Applies: well, single studies.
- Christ, 1975, Review and analysis of color coding research for visual displays, Human Factors 17(6), 542-570. Read: secondary. Finding: colour generally shortens search and locate times; effects on identification are smaller and mixed `?`. Cuts: supports coding for scanning. Applies: to locating items, not to naming them.
- Milroy and Poulton, 1978, as cited by sm86 2.4.3/3. Read: secondary (sm86 comment). Finding: curves labelled directly are read faster than curves keyed to a separate legend. Cuts: supports direct labels over legends. Applies: well.

### Accessibility requirements

- WCAG 2.2 SC 1.1.1 Non-text Content (A). URL: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-content.html . Read: Understanding page, through a summarising fetch. Requires a text alternative that is programmatically tied to the non-text content and serves the same purpose. It does not require that text to be visible. Purely decorative images must be ignorable by assistive technology. Cuts: neutral on visible labels. Applies: fully. A meaningful weather glyph needs an accessible name; a glyph that repeats adjacent visible text should be hidden.
- WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (A). URL: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html . Read: as above. Colour may not be the only visual means of conveying information; a shape or an icon satisfies it, text is one option among several. Cuts: supports symbols as the second cue. Applies: fully to status.
- WCAG 2.2 SC 1.3.3 Sensory Characteristics (A), and failure F26. URLs: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/sensory-characteristics.html , https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/failures/F26 . Read: as above. The criterion is about instructions: they must not rely only on shape, colour, size, position or sound. F26 is a failure where a graphical symbol conveys information and there is no other way at all to get that information; a text alternative is a sufficient other way. Cuts: neutral on visible labels. Applies: fully.
- Not required by any of the three: visible text beside a symbol. Not satisfied by hover alone: a hover title on a non-interactive element is unreachable by keyboard and touch (see NN/g 2014 below and ACT-011), so "name on hover" does not by itself meet the needs of sighted users who do not recognise the symbol.

## E. Design-system guidance

- Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Icons. URL: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/icons . Says interface icons help people understand items, actions and modes; they should be simple, use familiar visual metaphors tied directly to the content or action they stand for, and aim at a design most people recognise quickly. Advises against images that are hard to recognise across cultures or languages. Requires alternative text labels for custom icons, described as not visible and read out by VoiceOver. Says to put text inside an icon only when essential. Does not say when a symbol may stand without visible text for data or status.
- Apple Human Interface Guidelines, SF Symbols. URL: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols . Says a symbol can convey an object or concept wherever an icon can appear, including inside text, and that outline variants suit places where a symbol sits beside text. Requires alternative text labels for custom symbols. Nothing on standing alone.
- Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Accessibility and Charting data. URLs: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility , https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/charting-data . Accessibility: convey information with more than colour, for example distinct shapes or icons. Charting data: add descriptive text (titles, annotations, a one-line summary) to aid comprehension, and cites the Weather app's text summary as the example. So Apple's own guidance for a forecast display adds words to the graphic.
- IBM Carbon, Status indicators pattern. URL: https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/status-indicator-pattern/ . Says colour alone is insufficient for status. Says an icon alone can be effective if it meets contrast requirements, and still recommends pairing it with a label. Lists a descriptive text label as a required part of the icon indicator, and as required for shape indicators because bare shapes are less recognisable. For up or down change indicators, colour is optional when a sign, caret or arrow is present. The page gives two different counts (at least three of symbol, shape, colour, type in one place; at least two of colour, shape, symbol in another) and does not reconcile them.
- IBM Carbon, Pictograms. URL: https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/pictograms/usage/ . Treats pictograms as illustration, not as interface icons, and says not to use them in place of icons. Silent on standing alone and on text alternatives.
- GOV.UK. No icon guidance page found in the Design System. GDS blog, Retiring our icons (Moorhouse, 2013), URL: https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2013/06/18/retiring-our-icons/ : GOV.UK removed the icons that marked content types because testing showed people tried to click them, there was no evidence they made pages more memorable, and they were often confusing. A case of symbols for categories failing in testing.
- NHS digital service manual, Icons. URL: https://service-manual.nhs.uk/design-system/styles/icons . Use icons sparingly and never as decoration; a small tested set (arrows, search, tick and cross); the tick and cross sit under worded headings; the search icon without visible text must have an accessible name. Reports that tested icons were understood, and that testing was done in context on whole pages.
- ONS design system, Icons. URL: https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/design-system/foundations/icons . Always pair an icon with a text label, because a bare icon makes people think harder when they do not know it; icons should be universally understood; icons help people scan faster; decorative icons beside text are hidden from screen readers.
- Nielsen Norman Group, Icon Usability (Harley, 2014). URL: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/ . Already cited by ACT-002. Lists home, print and the search magnifier as the few near-universal icons; says labels should be visible at all times because hover labels fail on touch. Lists the real benefits of icons: compact, fast to recognise, no translation needed. Concerned with commands and navigation; says nothing about icons for objects, status or data.
- Material Design: not readable (see A). No statement recorded.

Summary of E: every system read that addresses the question pairs a status or category symbol with visible text by default. Carbon is the only one that states an icon alone can work, and it still recommends the label. None states the owner's position. None forbids it for conventional symbols either. All require a text alternative.

## F. What the evidence supports

1. A familiar symbol is recognised faster or more reliably than its word, for data and status: partly supported, weak to moderate. For finding and telling apart items on a screen, symbols beat words (NN/g 2016, one unreviewed study; Christ 1975 for colour; sm86 2.6/3). For trained symbol sets seen briefly, symbols beat words (Ells and Dewar 1979). For meaning, pictures of familiar objects are about as fast as words, not faster (Potter and Faulconer 1975 `?`), and are remembered better (picture superiority, HHS 14:15 ev 5). "More reliably" is contradicted wherever comprehension was measured cold: 45% for icons alone against 79% for words (Leung 2011), under 85% for standard safety symbols (Hancock 2004).
2. A value that carries its own unit or format needs no label: not supported; contradicted by guidance, untested by research. sm86 2.2/3 and 2.3/6 require the label and warn against relying on familiarity; no study was found either way. The unit names the dimension, not the quantity: degrees may be air temperature, feels-like, dew point or a bearing; a percentage beside a raindrop is misread even when spelled out (Gigerenzer 2005). The only support is that context can identify a field (sm86 2.2/3 comment; Wolff and Wogalter 1998 `?`) and that a unit may travel with the value (sm86 2.2/10).
3. Redundant labels slow people down or add nothing: weak and indirect. Clutter and crowding slow search (HHS 6:1, 6:6; Rosenholtz); one study found a second redundant highlighting scheme halved a gain (HHS 11:11); a unit need be stated once (sm86 2.3/11). Against: icon plus label performs as well as label alone and is preferred (Wiedenbeck 1999; Leung 2011), and added words improved understanding of charts (Ajani 2021). No study of a label beside a symbol in a data display was found. The sources' remedy for label clutter is quieter labels (sm86 2.2/8, 2.6/23), not none.
4. Symbols need a text alternative for assistive technology but not visible text: supported, strong, as a statement of what WCAG requires (1.1.1, 1.4.1, 1.3.3 with F26). It is a floor, not evidence that sighted users need no label; and a name on hover does not reach keyboard or touch users.
5. The set of truly widely understood symbols is small: supported, moderate to strong (NN/g 2014; Hancock 2004; Leung 2011; Zender and Cassedy 2014; standards bodies test every symbol). What gets in: direct depictions of physical things most people have met (sun, cloud, rain, snow, phone, clock, printer, house), arrows for direction and up or down, tick and cross, plus and minus, and symbol sets people are taught (road signs). What stays out: metaphors, anything standing for an abstract function or state, hazard symbols, brand-specific glyphs, and colour meanings, which sm86 2.6/32 ties to one society.
6. Removing non-data ink always helps: contradicted as stated; supported in part. Removal helps when the element did no work (Gillan and Richman 1994 `?`; Blasio and Bisantz 2002 `?`), has no measured benefit or is disliked in other cases (Inbar 2007; Ajani 2021), and embellishment can aid long-term recall at no cost in accuracy (Bateman 2010, n = 20).

COPY-016 support level: as written it bundles two claims of different standing. The symbol half (a conventional symbol in place of its word, name kept as a text alternative) merits `some-support`, with dissent recorded. The label-omission half (a self-identifying value carries no label) is `house-preference` and should record sm86:2.2/3 and sm86:2.3/6 as dissent. If the rule stays one record, `contested` is the honest level. Splitting it is better.

Sources for the symbol half: `sm86:2.4/13`, `sm86:2.4/12`, `sm86:2.6/3`, `sm86:2.6/5`, `sm86:2.6/16`, `hhs:14:15`, `hhs:14:8`, `nng:visual-indicators-differentiators`, `wcag22:1.1.1`, `wcag22:1.4.1`, `apple:Icons`, `carbon:patterns/status-indicator-pattern`, `owner:2026-11-16 depiction over words`; new registry entries needed for Ells and Dewar 1979, Leung et al. 2011, Hancock et al. 2004, Isherwood et al. 2007. Sources for the label half: `owner:2026-11-16 depiction over words`, `sm86:2.2/10`, `sm86:2.3/11`, `sm86:2.0/4`. Drop `sm86:2.0/3` from the list or keep it only as general background: it is about not making users convert or look things up, which cuts both ways. `hhs:14:2` is about clickable images and belongs in dissent, not support.

Limits the evidence demands that the rule does not yet state:
- The symbol must depict the thing itself, not a metaphor for it, and the audience must already know it; where that is in doubt the rule requires a test or a visible label (sm86 2.4/13, 2.6/5; Leung 2011; Isherwood 2007). Audiences that include older adults or first-time and infrequent users get visible labels more readily (Leung 2011; Hancock 2004; HHS 14:2 comment).
- A symbol names the kind of thing, never the amount, likelihood or duration; the number beside it must say what it measures when more than one reading is plausible. A percentage beside a raindrop needs "chance" or "humidity"; degrees need the scale where the audience is mixed (Gigerenzer 2005; Reed et al.; sm86 2.2/10).
- A label may be dropped only when the display's context leaves one reading; a value shown outside that context (a lone tile, an export, a notification) keeps its label (sm86 2.2/3 comment).
- "Name available on hover" is insufficient: the name must be a programmatic text alternative, and reachable without a pointer; if the symbol repeats visible text it is hidden from assistive technology instead (WCAG 1.1.1; NN/g 2014).
- Meaning must not rest on colour, and conventional colour meanings are not universal (sm86 2.6/30, 2.6/32; WCAG 1.4.1). Pictures are not used as a measure of quantity (sm86 2.4.4/11).
- The why line should not say a symbol is "read faster than the word" without qualification; the supportable claims are faster to find when scanning, as fast to understand when familiar, better remembered, and independent of language.

Further rules the evidence suggests and the library appears to lack:
- A symbol, colour or abbreviation that this product gives a meaning of its own is defined on the same screen, in a short key that shows the code itself. Sources: `sm86:2.6/6`, `sm86:2.4/12`, `sm86:2.0/21`, `hhs:11:9`. Support: some-support.
- A symbol or colour keeps one meaning across the product, and a symbol reserved for a critical state is used for nothing else. Sources: `sm86:2.4/12`, `sm86:2.6/7`, `sm86:2.6/13`, `sm86:2.6/31`. Support: practice-consensus. (Check overlap with the rule that already cites sm86:2.6/7.)
- Series, segments and categories in a chart or map are labelled directly on the mark; a separate legend is used only when direct labels will not fit, and then in the same order as the marks. Sources: `sm86:2.4.3/3`, `sm86:2.4.3/4`, `sm86:2.4.4/8`, `sm86:2.4.5/2`, `sm86:2.4.8/4`, Ajani et al. 2021. Support: some-support.
- A unit or label shared by a whole row, column or group is stated once for the group, not beside every value. Sources: `sm86:2.3/11`, `sm86:2.4.4/8` comment. Support: practice-consensus. This is the evidenced form of "reduce labels" and could replace the label-omission half of COPY-016.
- Labels are visually quieter than the data they name. Sources: `sm86:2.2/8`, `sm86:2.6/23`, `sm86:2.3/8`. Support: practice-consensus.
- Status is shown by a symbol or shape plus colour, and by a word as well wherever the symbol is not one of the conventional few. Sources: `sm86:2.6/30`, `wcag22:1.4.1`, `carbon:patterns/status-indicator-pattern`, `nng:visual-indicators-differentiators`. Support: some-support. (Check overlap with existing colour rules.)
- A new or product-specific pictogram is shown to representative users before it is relied on without a label. Sources: `sm86:2.4/13`, `hhs:14:8`, ISO 9186 and ANSI Z535.3 as method. Support: practice-consensus.
